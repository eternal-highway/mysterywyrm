## 2. The conceit

The spine of the project is the **Old English Rune Poem** — the Anglo-Saxon poem
that gives one riddling stanza to each of the 29 runes of the futhorc. Tonkin's
claim is that this poem *is* an alphabet book, and an alphabet song: rune carvers
likely sang it, because setting a list to music makes it memorable.

*Letters for Titles* is then described as "an alphabet song sung to the tune of
the Old English Rune Poem" — and, in the site's own framing, as simultaneously:

- **an instruction manual**,
- **a book of divination**,
- a thing that "writes content in code, and hides material in it like Old English
  manuscripts did."

The letters are presented as ones "of such power they moved an ancient society to
act, to war, to justice, to joy, to survival."

## 3. Structural devices

Four structural ideas recur and appear to be the actual architecture of the work,
not decoration:

### 3.1 Begin in the middle
> "If you have just found this Alphabet Book, this is not the beginning, this is
> the middle. You are in the middle."

The entry point is deliberately not stanza 1. See `/the-middle/`.

### 3.2 The mirror fold (the "rune code")
Take the 29 runes, lay them in a row, then **fold the row in half**: 1 against
29, 2 against 28, and so on. Read each pair together. `/the-middle/` describes
the runes "bowing to themselves in the mirror, from the ends to the middle and
back again." This pairing is the site's central cipher and the reason the book
starts mid-sequence.

### 3.3 The lunar calendar
The poem is read as a moon-month: one rune per night of the waxing moon up to
full, then the sequence turns and retraces itself back out to darkness — the
mirror fold expressed as time rather than as a list.

### 3.4 Octaves
The futhorc is also read musically: **three rows of eight (three octaves), plus
added vowels to reach 29**. Feoh (wealth) is "1.1, the first note in the first
octave." See `/octave/`.

## 4. Divination

The site grounds rune-casting in etymology: Old English *rún* means **secret**,
and the secret is that these letters were not only for writing — they were carved
into wood and cast as lots to read fates, answer questions, solve problems. Each
stanza is treated as a riddle carrying a warning or a picture of life, and the
poem itself points at *tanas* — twigs for divination.

This is worked out in a **Rune Casting** series of pages (e.g. `Rune Casting: Os`,
`Rune Casting: Ear`).

## 5. Close-reading method

Two examples show how the essays actually operate — the argument is usually made
from the physical page of the manuscript, not from paraphrase:

- **Wyrd** (`/wyrd/`) — *wyrd* is glossed as "judgement and fate, also glory and
  repute." The essay notes that the word *gesingan* ("to sing") is broken across
  a line-end just after its N, which drops the **Ior** rune — the beaver rune —
  into the middle of the word. Beavers sing; so the wisdom about earning an
  interpretation of one's doom "sings its song very comfortably in the Beaver
  chapter."
- **Byþ** (`/byth/`) — *byþ* is the first word of nearly every stanza; the essay
  uses it to open Old English temporality (which is also what took Tonkin to Old
  English in the first place — see §7).

- **Ear / The Grave** (`/translating-ear/`, `/the-grave/`) — stanza 29 is read as
  waves made of earth rather than water. The riddle's answer is *the grave*, and
  the stanza is marked as formally set apart: "when you get to Ear, you know it's
  the end."

Also indexed: an essay on **Wynn** (ᚹ) tracing how scribes settled on UU for the
/w/ sound, how post-Roman England kept the rune instead, and how it rounded into
the quill-friendly Ƿ.

## 7. The author

From the site's own bio page (`/vern-tonkin/`):

- Spanish was Tonkin's first language; the family moved to the States while
  Tonkin was a toddler and the world became English-only.
- College Spanish went badly — a D− in third-year Spanish, from an Argentine
  professor who could not fathom a student from Peru speaking at a toddler's
  level.
- In grad school the choice was one more year of Spanish for the PhD, or Old
  English. Tonkin took Old English poetry — having come to study **temporality**.
- Affiliated with the **University of Washington**. Also listed on Academia.edu.

Tonkin's stated view of the source text: the Rune Poem is the odd one out in the
Old English corpus. Where the rest is elegiac and grave, this one is "upbeat and
almost optimistic — comparable to Sesame Street."

## 9. Background on the source text (not from the site)

Included for grounding, since the whole project rests on it:

The Old English Rune Poem gives 29 stanzas to the runes of the Anglo-Saxon
futhorc, each a short riddling verse on the rune's name (Feoh = cattle/wealth,
Ac = oak, Ger = year/harvest, Ear = grave). It is one of three surviving rune
poems, alongside the Norwegian and Icelandic. Its manuscript — Cotton Otho B.x —
burned in the 1731 Ashburnham House fire; the poem survives because George Hickes
printed a transcription in his *Thesaurus* of 1705. Every modern reading,
including this one, descends from that printed copy rather than from the
manuscript.

That transmission history is worth holding next to Tonkin's claims about hidden
structure: arguments from line breaks and letter placement (as in `/wyrd/`) rest
on Hickes's typeset page, not on the lost original — which is a real question to
put to the work, and one the site may well already address on a page not indexed.
