## Pair pressure

The chapter does not offer a neat opposition between destructive hail and restorative water. Hægl falls quickly, batters, and melts; Lagu prolongs terror, suspends arrival, and strips the traveler of control. Water is both the form into which violence passes and the medium in which danger continues. Even calm appears after, not outside, tempest.

Its sounds also resist a purely meteorological reading. Hægl is breath forced through a narrow aperture, ghosted or voiced depending on position. Lagu is lateral resonance, recitation, tempo, and a flag without wind. The chapter moves between weather and utterance: storm becomes argument, hymn, protest, code, translation, and the book's own account of how its compositions link.

The theology is equally irreducible. *How to Go Overboard* receives Jonah's God as present on land and sea, pursuing refusal into the storm; *H is for Hægl* places unstable selfhood before a divine voice in the whirlwind. *A Horrible Wonder* brings the Beowulf mere's bloody fire-water and unknowable depth into the cycle. Recurrence here does not abolish judgement, death, terror, or grief.

## Crossing relations

Hægl's formal adjacency to Nyd remains separate from its reflection with Lagu: the two short stanzas accelerate twelve stresses into two lines, and hail can precipitate need. Lagu also participates in the Mann → Lagu → Ing → Eþel tempo sequence recorded elsewhere. Neither crossing displaces the reflected Hægl/Lagu chapter pair.

*L is for Letters for Titles* is unusually important structural testimony. It describes the work as an alphabet book written forward in real time while linking backward in retrospective arrangement, a mirror within a mirror, and a collection of interconnected compositions in multiple streams. The reconstructed braid supports that account, but does not exhaust it.
